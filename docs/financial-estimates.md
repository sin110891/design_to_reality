# Financial Estimates: Determinism-First Agentic Design Room

**Status:** Planning model, not investment advice  
**Geography:** United Kingdom first  
**Currency:** GBP, excluding VAT  
**Forecast horizon:** Five years from commercial launch

---

## 1. Executive conclusion

The concept can support a credible UK vertical-software business. A realistic five-year objective is:

```text
Base case:
  350–500 paying firms
  £8,000–£10,000 blended annual contract value
  £3m–£5m exit ARR
  70%–80% software gross margin
```

In three years, **£0.7m–£1.5m ARR** is realistically achievable if the company proves a narrow workflow, converts paid pilots into recurring contracts, and reduces founder-led onboarding.

In five years, **£2m–£5m ARR** is a credible base range. Reaching **£8m–£12m ARR** is possible but should be treated as an upside case requiring multi-site customers, channel distribution, or transaction revenue—not simply more small design studios.

The current planning market sizes are:

```text
UK TAM:                 approximately £140m–£220m annual software revenue
Initial UK SAM:         approximately £20m–£40m annual software revenue
Realistic Year-5 SOM:   approximately £3m–£5m ARR
```

These estimates exclude procurement commissions from the base case.

The strongest initial commercial wedge is likely:

> **Move from room brief to client-approved, purchasable concept faster.**

Interior designers are the easiest design-led entry point, but kitchen/bathroom firms and design-and-build firms can support higher annual contract values once deterministic catalogue, geometry, scope, and pricing tools are credible.

## 2. What is being monetised

The product is not best priced as an image generator. Low-cost AI rendering tools make that position difficult to defend.

The paid outcome is a professional project workspace that combines:

- brief capture and design exploration;
- firm-specific style and reference memory;
- rapid, controlled revisions;
- client comparison and approval;
- real-product sourcing and selection;
- versioned schedules and evidence;
- later, spatial validation, scope, and indicative pricing.

Different segments buy different outcomes:

| Segment | Primary economic benefit |
|---------|--------------------------|
| Interior designers | Concept, sourcing, and revision hours saved; additional project capacity |
| Kitchen/bathroom firms | Better appointment conversion; faster design-to-quote; fewer configuration omissions |
| Design-and-build firms | Faster scope/quote creation; omissions caught; margin protection |
| Architects | Faster feasibility options; fewer projects failing when tender exceeds budget |

The deterministic/agentic architecture supports this value proposition:

- agent interaction reduces training and coordination burden;
- deterministic tools create professional trust;
- firm memory increases retention and switching cost;
- approvals and provenance make the output usable in real work.

## 3. Market evidence and limitations

Available market counts vary because official SIC codes are broader than the actual target customer.

Relevant indicators:

- [IBISWorld estimates 6,415 UK interior-design businesses in 2026](https://www.ibisworld.com/united-kingdom/industry/interior-design-activities/14549/).
- [IBISWorld estimates 16,409 UK architectural-activity businesses in 2025](https://www.ibisworld.com/united-kingdom/number-of-businesses/architectural-activities/3925/), but most are not target residential practices.
- [RIBA reports that practices with 1–9 staff comprise 78% of Chartered Practices](https://www.ribaj.com/intelligence/riba-business-benchmarking-survey-2025/), supporting a large small-practice segment.
- [IBISWorld estimates roughly 60,000 UK residential-building businesses](https://www.ibisworld.com/united-kingdom/industry/residential-building-construction/2411/), but only a minority are design-led, digitally mature, and suitable for this product.
- [The UK government reports 885,000 construction SMEs in 2025](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/business-population-estimates-2025/business-population-estimates-for-the-uk-and-regions-2025-statistical-release), demonstrating fragmentation rather than an immediately serviceable market.
- [The 2025 fitted-kitchen market was approximately £5.4bn across about 1.05 million installations](https://www.kbbreview.com/76975/research/independents-lead-the-way-as-kitchen-market-shows-resilience/); independent studios represented almost 40% of market value.
- [A kbbreview survey found 77% of independent kitchen/bathroom retailers were single-site showrooms](https://www.kbbreview.com/59178/research/the-kbbreview-retail-survey-2024-business-health/), indicating a fragmented owner-led buyer base.

Therefore:

- broad sector counts substantially overstate the addressable market;
- some firms sit in multiple segments, creating double counting;
- many small firms have low software budgets or insufficient project volume;
- the correct initial market is firms with repeated residential projects, client-facing design work, and measurable time/conversion pain.

The figures below use filtered planning assumptions rather than treating every registered business as a potential customer.

## 4. TAM: total addressable market

### 4.1 Bottom-up segment assumptions

| Segment | Planning firm count | Plausible annual contract | Implied annual market |
|---------|--------------------:|--------------------------:|----------------------:|
| Interior-design studios | 5,000–6,400 | £6,000–£7,200 | £30m–£46m |
| Small residential architecture practices | 3,000–5,000 | £7,500–£10,000 | £23m–£50m |
| Independent kitchen/bathroom firms | 3,000–6,000 | £9,000–£15,000 | £27m–£90m |
| Design-led residential builders/design-and-build firms | 7,000–10,000 | £10,000–£15,000 | £70m–£150m |

The raw sum is approximately **£150m–£336m annually**.

This cannot be used directly because:

- businesses overlap across architecture, interiors, kitchens, and construction;
- not every firm needs every workspace;
- microbusinesses may only support a lower-priced tier;
- some firms will remain committed to incumbent CAD/project systems;
- several deterministic capabilities are future scope rather than current product.

Applying overlap and product-readiness discounts produces a more responsible planning TAM:

> **UK software TAM: approximately £140m–£220m ARR.**

### 4.2 Conservative cross-check

The existing business analysis estimated approximately 24,000 relevant firms. At a flat £6,000 annual contract:

```text
24,000 × £6,000 = £144m annual software TAM
```

This supports using **£140m** as the conservative headline rather than the larger raw segment total.

### 4.3 Design-only TAM

If the product remains primarily a concept and sourcing tool for interior designers, the addressable UK market is materially smaller:

```text
5,000–6,400 firms × £3,000–£7,200 ACV
= approximately £15m–£46m ARR TAM
```

This can still produce a good software business, but probably not a large venture outcome in the UK alone.

### 4.4 Expanded platform TAM

The broader £140m–£220m TAM depends on adding reliable role packs:

- kitchen configuration, catalogues, dimensions, and quoting;
- builder scope, quantities, work packages, and rate controls;
- architectural feasibility, evidence, and stage approvals.

The agentic UI helps make these workflows accessible, but it does not create this TAM by itself. The deterministic domain engines unlock each segment.

## 5. SAM: serviceable available market

The first serviceable market should be narrower:

- England first, especially London, South East, and major affluent cities;
- premium residential refurbishment and interiors;
- firms with approximately 2–30 staff;
- repeated concept, sourcing, feasibility, or quoting work;
- willingness to share references and project corrections;
- adequate project value to justify £500–£1,500 monthly software spend.

Planning segment:

```text
3,000–5,000 suitable firms
× £6,000–£10,000 blended ACV
= £18m–£50m
```

After allowing for overlap and practical product fit:

> **Initial UK SAM: approximately £20m–£40m ARR.**

This SAM should not initially include every segment equally.

A sensible sequence is:

1. Interior-design studios: prove design quality, sourcing, revision, and approval value.
2. Kitchen/bathroom studios or design-and-build firms: prove measurable commercial value.
3. Architecture practices: add feasibility and evidence-led budget ranges.
4. Multi-site and larger accounts: add administration, permissions, integrations, and standardisation.

## 6. SOM: realistically obtainable market

### Three-year SOM

A credible three-year base:

```text
100–180 firms
× £7,000–£9,000 ACV
= £0.7m–£1.6m ARR
```

This represents only about 2%–8% of the initial SAM.

### Five-year SOM

A credible five-year base:

```text
350–500 firms
× £8,000–£10,000 ACV
= £2.8m–£5m ARR
```

This is approximately 7%–25% of the estimated initial SAM. The high end requires expansion beyond founder-network sales.

### Why this is achievable

- the target industries are fragmented and poorly integrated;
- the product addresses repeated, expensive professional work;
- one saved project/revision or one caught omission can justify the annual price;
- firm-specific style and project memory support retention;
- a shared platform can serve multiple role-specific workspaces;
- paid pilots can produce strong reference selling.

### Why it is not automatic

- small professional firms buy software slowly and churn when project volumes fall;
- design quality alone is easy to compare with low-cost image tools;
- each segment has different workflows and vocabulary;
- onboarding proprietary references may remain labour-intensive;
- kitchens/builders need deterministic functionality not yet implemented;
- professional trust requires evidence over many projects;
- acquiring hundreds of fragmented firms needs partnerships, channels, or an effective sales team.

## 7. Recommended pricing model

### 7.1 Pilot pricing

Start with paid workflow studies:

| Pilot | Indicative price | Scope |
|-------|-----------------:|-------|
| Design pilot | £1,500–£2,500 | 3–5 live projects over 6–8 weeks |
| Commercial workflow pilot | £2,500–£5,000 | Design + sourcing/quote workflow + baseline measurement |
| Multi-site pilot | £7,500–£20,000 | Several users/sites, integrations, success criteria |

The pilot should measure baseline versus after:

- time to first concept;
- concept/revision hours;
- client approval time;
- retained generated products/scope;
- conversion for showrooms;
- omissions or margin protected for builders.

### 7.2 Recurring pricing

| Plan | Indicative price | Intended customer |
|------|-----------------:|-------------------|
| Professional | £199–£399/month | Solo designer or small studio |
| Studio | £499–£899/month | Collaborative interior/architecture team |
| Sales & specification | £999–£1,999/month | Kitchen/bathroom or design-and-build firm |
| Multi-site / enterprise | £20,000–£75,000/year | Groups, developers, national showrooms |

Potential one-time onboarding:

```text
£1,000–£5,000
```

for importing reference libraries, establishing firm profiles, configuring catalogues, and training the team.

Onboarding should not become the primary revenue stream; repeated manual customisation would indicate consultancy rather than scalable software.

### 7.3 Competitive price context

- [Houzz Pro advertises £139/month in the UK](https://www.houzz.co.uk/houzz-pro/pricing) for a broad contractor/design workflow product.
- [Studio Designer charges $69–$109 per user/month](https://www.studiodesigner.com/pricing/).
- [Programa starts around $71/month plus additional seats](https://programa.design/pricing).
- [DesignFiles starts around $55–$75/month](https://designfiles.co/plans).
- Basic UK builder quoting tools often charge approximately £19–£50/month, while [QuoteWise advertises £289/month for AI drawing estimates and £1,499/month for teams](https://quotewise.build/).
- Specialist kitchen platforms such as [Winner Flex](https://www.cyncly.com/en-GB/products/winner-flex) use sales-led custom pricing.

Implication:

> A design-only image tool cannot sustain £500–£1,500/month merely because it uses AI.

Higher pricing requires measurable workflow value: proprietary firm memory, controlled revisions, client approvals, real products, configuration, schedules, scope, or pricing.

### 7.4 Usage and compute controls

Avoid unlimited expensive generation.

Each plan should include:

- a number of active projects;
- a pooled image/agent credit allowance;
- deterministic/local previews where suitable;
- paid high-resolution or deep-exploration overages;
- transparent limits for 3D reconstruction and rendering.

This protects gross margin while keeping simple actions fast and effectively free.

## 8. Five-year base financial model

### 8.1 Assumptions

- paid pilots precede annual contracts;
- early customers are founder-sold;
- onboarding becomes progressively standardised;
- blended ACV rises as the mix shifts from solo designers to teams and commercial workflows;
- procurement commission is excluded;
- gross margin improves through deterministic routing, model choice, caching, and usage limits;
- recognised revenue is below exit ARR during growth because customers are added throughout each year.

### 8.2 Base case

| Year | Exit paying firms | Blended ACV | Exit ARR | Approx. recognised revenue | Target gross margin | Approx. gross profit |
|-----:|------------------:|------------:|---------:|-----------------------------:|--------------------:|---------------------:|
| 1 | 15 | £6,000 | £90k | £60k–£80k | 65%–70% | £40k–£55k |
| 2 | 55 | £6,500 | £358k | £220k–£280k | 70%–75% | £155k–£210k |

| 4 | 270 | £8,500 | £2.30m | £1.6m–£1.9m | 75%–80% | £1.2m–£1.5m |

This is a demanding but plausible trajectory. It requires:

- strong pilot outcomes;
- repeatable onboarding by Year 2;
- a clear winning segment;
- annual gross retention above roughly 80%;
- sales channels beyond founder relationships by Years 3–4;
- controlled inference and customer-success costs.

### 9.1 Year-5 scenarios

The base case is the most defensible planning range.

- national kitchen/showroom groups;
- supplier/manufacturer distribution;
- developer or build-to-rent accounts;
- white-label/API distribution;
- successful entry into additional English-speaking markets;
- procurement or transaction revenue.

It should not be forecast simply by assuming more outbound sales to small firms.

### 9.2 Segment-mix sensitivity

| Product outcome | Likely blended ACV | Five-year implication |
|-----------------|--------------------:|-----------------------|
| AI concepts only | £1,500–£3,500 | High competition; difficult to exceed £1m–£2m UK ARR |

This is the most important financial conclusion:

> The agentic interface may improve adoption, but deterministic commercial depth determines ACV and market size.

## 10. Optional transaction revenue

Procurement can expand the business beyond software subscriptions, but it should not be assumed until provider agreements, attribution, and customer behaviour are proven.

Illustrative only:

```text
£100m annual product GMV influenced through the platform
× 1.5% net referral/commission
= £1.5m annual transaction revenue
```

The kitchen market provides scale:

- fitted-kitchen product sales were reported at approximately £5.4bn;
- independent studios represented almost 40% of value.

However, influencing product selection does not automatically create commission entitlement. The company would need:

- supplier/affiliate agreements;
- clear disclosure;
- reliable order attribution;
- returns/cancellation treatment;
- price neutrality and professional conflict controls.

Base forecasts therefore exclude transaction revenue.

## 11. Unit economics and gross margin

### Target cost structure

| Cost category | Mature target as % of revenue |
|---------------|------------------------------:|
| Model/image inference | 5%–12% |
| Hosting, storage, search | 2%–5% |
| Direct onboarding/support | 8%–15% |
| Total COGS | 18%–30% |
| Gross margin | 70%–82% |

Gross margin will be weaker if:

- customers generate unlimited high-resolution images;
- Gemini-style edits are repeatedly chained;
- every request invokes an agent;
- firm libraries require continuous manual curation;
- customer projects need bespoke taxonomies or workflows.

It improves when:

- direct manipulation and calculations remain deterministic;
- generation uses low-resolution previews before refinement;
- requests are routed to the cheapest proven model;
- outputs and embeddings are cached;
- local composite/3D render paths replace repeated generative edits;
- onboarding follows a standard import → annotate → approve profile workflow.

### Value-to-price check

The annual price should capture approximately 10%–25% of provable customer value.

Examples from the existing business analysis:

- an interior studio saving roughly £1,700 per project across eight projects creates about £13,600 annual value;
- a builder saving £600 per quote across several quotes per month can create tens of thousands in annual capacity;
- a showroom gaining one additional kitchen sale can cover much of an annual contract;
- one material omission caught on a large refurbishment can exceed the subscription price.

These hypotheses must be measured in paid pilots before setting final tiers.

This can become either:

### Capital-efficient vertical SaaS

```text
£1m–£2m ARR
lean team
high founder/customer proximity
potential path to break-even
```

This is achievable without maximising venture funding if onboarding and inference are controlled.

### Venture-backed workflow platform

```text
£5m–£12m+ ARR
multi-site accounts
several role packs
international/channel distribution
transaction or API revenue
```

This requires more product breadth, integrations, sales capacity, and capital.

At approximately £4m ARR and 75%–80% gross margin, the company would generate roughly £3m–£3.2m gross profit before product, sales, and administrative operating expenses. That can support a meaningful 15–25 person organisation, but profitability would depend on sales efficiency and product scope.

## 13. Milestones that de-risk the forecast

### Before treating £1m ARR as credible

- two or more completed paid pilots;
- at least one renewal or annual conversion;
- measurable time, conversion, or margin outcome;
- at least 50% of produced concepts/structured outputs retained with limited correction;
- onboarding requiring less than one working day per firm;
- evidence customers use the tool on a second live project.

### Before treating £3m–£5m ARR as credible

- one segment with repeatable sales messaging;
- 20–30 paying firms and strong reference customers;
- gross retention above approximately 80%;
- blended ACV above £6,000;
- stable cost per accepted design action;
- a repeatable channel or sales process;
- firm profile/reference onboarding mostly self-service;
- agent suggestions demonstrably improve outcomes rather than only engagement.

### Before treating £8m–£12m ARR as credible

- multi-site or enterprise contracts;
- strong net revenue retention from seats, workflows, or volume;
- proven integration into daily project records;
- procurement/API/channel revenue or international expansion;
- deterministic kitchen/build/geometry capabilities beyond image generation.

## 14. Main financial risks

### Commoditised generation

If customers view the product as a rendering wrapper, pricing compresses toward £20–£150/month.

**Mitigation:** firm memory, revisions, real products, project record, approvals, geometry, and commercial workflows.

### Segment sprawl

Serving designers, architects, kitchens, and builders simultaneously can create four partial products.

**Mitigation:** enter through one design-led wedge, then add role packs only after shared-core reuse is proven.

### Consultancy-heavy onboarding

Manual reference curation and bespoke prompts can produce revenue but poor software margins.

**Mitigation:** standardise onboarding and track human hours per firm.

### Weak retention

Small firms have uneven project volumes and may subscribe only during busy periods.

**Mitigation:** annual/project bundles, durable project records, firm libraries, team collaboration, and ongoing sourcing/procurement value.

### Inference cost and latency

Agent loops and image generation can destroy both experience and margin.

**Mitigation:** determinism-first routing, strict budgets, credits, caching, and selective refinement.

### Professional liability and trust

Incorrect geometry, pricing, or fit claims can prevent adoption or create liability.

**Mitigation:** separate visual, inferred, measured, commercial, and approved states; never allow an agent to certify unsupported claims.

## 15. Recommended commercial focus

### First 12–18 months

Focus on one primary buyer:

> Small premium interior-design or design-and-build firms producing repeated client concepts and sourced schedules.

Sell paid pilots at £1,500–£3,000 and identify whether the strongest repeatable value is:

- design/revision time;
- client approval;
- sourcing/procurement;
- conversion;
- scope/quote preparation.

Do not commit the roadmap to all four segments until paid behaviour shows which outcome drives renewal.

### Years 2–3

- convert the winning pilot into a repeatable annual plan;
- introduce firm style/reference onboarding;
- add one adjacent role pack;
- target £0.7m–£1.5m ARR;
- establish partnerships with suppliers, trade bodies, or software channels.

### Years 4–5

- expand to multi-site or larger accounts;
- add deterministic commercial/spatial depth;
- reach 350–500 paying firms or equivalent enterprise revenue;
- target £3m–£5m ARR;
- introduce transaction revenue only after software retention is proven.

## 16. Final assessment

### Realistically achievable

```text
Three years:
  £0.7m–£1.5m ARR

Five years:
  £2m–£5m ARR

Potential upside:
  £8m–£12m ARR
```

The realistic base is a good vertical-software company: fundable by angels and specialist funds, potentially profitable, and capable of becoming strategically valuable.

It is not yet an obvious large venture-scale company because:

- the UK SAM is finite;
- small-firm sales are fragmented;
- current visible value still depends heavily on frontier image models;
- the higher-value deterministic engines remain future work.

The route to a larger outcome is not “more agent.” It is:

1. prove that design quality and speed cause customers to pay and renew;
2. capture firm-specific memory and decisions;
3. connect design to real products and approvals;
4. add deterministic configuration, scope, pricing, or spatial validation;
5. move up-market or add transaction/channel revenue.

The core financial thesis is:

> **Agent-first interaction improves adoption; deterministic professional workflows create ACV, trust, retention, and defensibility.**
